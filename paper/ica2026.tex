\begin{abstract}
Plant disease classifiers trained on laboratory imagery report accuracies above
$0.99$, yet field deployment disappoints. The size of that gap is widely
asserted and rarely measured under conditions that make the measurement
trustworthy. We report a cross-domain evaluation in which the two obvious
confounds are closed rather than assumed away. First, train/test contamination:
an exhaustive perceptual-hash search over every distinct-hash pair across the
two corpora, followed by human adjudication of all \NLeakNearCore{} near
candidates, establishes \NLeakExactCore{} exact overlaps between the
laboratory training corpus and the field evaluation corpus. Second, label-space
construction: the shared \NSharedClasses{}-class space is assembled only from a
deterministic normaliser applied identically to both label sets and from a
previously recorded human policy decision, so no new semantic judgement is
introduced to make the comparison look better or worse. Three ImageNet-pretrained
backbones are trained under one protocol on \NPv{} laboratory images and
evaluated on \NXdImages{} field images, with paired significance tests and
bias-corrected accelerated bootstrap intervals. Every input is bound to a
digest-verified dataset lock (\LockDigest{}). We find that near-saturated
in-domain accuracy carries almost no information about which backbone transfers,
and that a temperature fitted on in-domain validation data---standard practice
for calibration---makes out-of-domain calibration substantially worse rather
than better.

\section{Introduction}
% =========================================================================== %

Image-based plant disease recognition has an unusually clean success story and
an unusually persistent complaint. The success story is PlantVillage
\cite{mohanty2016plantvillage}: tens of thousands of labelled leaf photographs
on which convolutional networks reach accuracies above $0.99$, a result
reproduced so widely that the benchmark is effectively saturated. The complaint
is that these accuracies do not survive contact with a real field. PlantVillage
images are single detached leaves photographed against uniform backgrounds under
controlled lighting. A photograph taken by a grower contains occlusion, soil,
multiple leaves, variable illumination, and a camera held at whatever distance
was convenient.

That a gap exists is not in dispute, and field-condition corpora have been built
precisely to expose it \cite{singh2020plantdoc,moupojou2023fieldplant,wei2024plantwild}.
What is harder to find is a measurement of the gap that a sceptical reader can
trust, because two confounds sit between the raw comparison and any conclusion
drawn from it.

The first is contamination. If any field evaluation image is a duplicate or
near-duplicate of a laboratory training image, the measured transfer is
optimistic by an unknown amount. This is not hypothetical: near-duplicate
contamination has been documented in benchmarks far more scrutinised than these
\cite{barz2020purging}. Most cross-corpus plant disease comparisons do not
report a leakage audit at all.

The second is the label space. The two corpora do not share a taxonomy, so any
cross-corpus evaluation must construct a correspondence between them. That
construction is a research degree of freedom. A generous mapping that pairs
biologically similar classes will report a smaller gap than a conservative one,
and the reader usually cannot tell which was used or what was excluded.

This paper closes both. Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A leakage-controlled evaluation protocol.} Candidate duplicate
pairs are generated by exhaustive perceptual-hash comparison over every
distinct-hash pair across the two corpora---not by an approximate index---and
every candidate is adjudicated by a human. Perceptual hashing generates
candidates; it never decides. The result is \NLeakExactCore{} exact and
\NLeakNearCore{} adjudicated-distinct near pairs between the corpora, so the
measured degradation is a generalisation measurement rather than an artefact.

\item \textbf{An evidence-based shared label space.} The
\NSharedClasses{}-class cross-domain space is assembled from exactly two
sources: a deterministic normaliser applied unchanged to both label sets, and a
human policy decision recorded before this evaluation was designed. Every
excluded class carries a written reason, including biologically plausible
near-misses that we decline to assert. The mapping is conservative by
construction and we say what a more generous one would change.

\item \textbf{A controlled multi-seed comparison with paired statistics.} Three
backbones spanning a $15\times$ parameter range are trained under one protocol
across independent seeds, evaluated in-domain and cross-domain, and compared
with McNemar's exact test on per-item correctness \cite{mcnemar1947,dietterich1998tests}
and bias-corrected accelerated bootstrap intervals \cite{efron1987bca}, with
family-wise error control \cite{holm1979}.

\item \textbf{Two findings that matter for practice.} In-domain ranking on a
saturated benchmark does not predict cross-domain ranking; and a temperature
fitted on in-domain validation data, applied across the domain shift, degrades
calibration rather than repairing it---the opposite of the standard
recommendation.
\end{enumerate}

Every input is bound to a digest-verified dataset lock, every reported number is
generated from run artefacts by script, and no number in this document was typed
by hand.

\section{Related Work}
% =========================================================================== %

\subsubsection{Laboratory corpora and their saturation.}
PlantVillage \cite{mohanty2016plantvillage} established the standard benchmark
and, with it, the standard result: accuracies above $0.99$ across dozens of
crop--disease classes. The corpus's construction---detached leaves, uniform
backgrounds---is stated plainly in the original work. What followed was a long
sequence of papers reporting incremental gains on a benchmark with very little
headroom, a pattern that makes in-domain comparison progressively less
informative.

\subsubsection{Field-condition corpora.}
PlantDoc \cite{singh2020plantdoc} was assembled specifically to test whether
laboratory-trained models transfer, and reported that they largely do not.
FieldPlant \cite{moupojou2023fieldplant} and PlantWild \cite{wei2024plantwild}
extend the field-condition setting to larger and more varied imagery, and
PlantSeg \cite{wei2025plantseg} adds segmentation supervision. Our work is
complementary: rather than proposing a new corpus or a stronger model, we ask
what conditions a cross-corpus comparison must satisfy before its number means
anything.

\subsubsection{Generalisation and dataset bias.}
The observation that models absorb corpus-specific regularities rather than the
intended concept predates deep learning \cite{torralba2011bias} and has been
quantified repeatedly since \cite{recht2019imagenet,geirhos2020shortcut}. For
plant disease, the uniform background of laboratory imagery is an obvious
shortcut candidate: it is perfectly correlated with the corpus and trivially
easy to represent. Near-duplicate leakage is the adjacent failure, and
\cite{barz2020purging} showed it survives in benchmarks that had been studied
for a decade. We treat both as things to measure, not to assume.

\subsubsection{Calibration and selective prediction.}
Modern networks are systematically over-confident, and temperature scaling is
the standard single-parameter remedy \cite{guo2017calibration}. Under
distribution shift, uncertainty estimates degrade further and the ranking of
calibration methods changes \cite{ovadia2019trust}. Selective prediction
\cite{geifman2017selective} offers the deployment-relevant view: not how
accurate a model is, but how accurate it is on the subset it is willing to act
on. All three matter for an agricultural decision system, where an unreliable
confident prediction can cost a season.

\subsubsection{Positioning within this venue.}
LeADS \cite{das2025leads} carries a leaf-image pipeline through stress and
disease detection to severity estimation, establishing the applied trajectory
this community is on. AgroBench \cite{shinoda2025agrobench} shows how far
general-purpose vision--language models remain from reliable agricultural
judgement. Our contribution sits underneath both: whatever the downstream stage,
its evaluation inherits whatever contamination and label-space slack exist in
the cross-domain measurement it rests on. We aim to make that foundation
auditable.

\section{Datasets and Leakage Control}
% =========================================================================== %

\subsection{Corpora}

\textbf{PlantVillage} supplies the laboratory domain: \NPv{} colour images over
\NPvClasses{} crop--disease classes, split \NPvTrain{} train / \NPvTest{} test.
The corpus is pinned to an immutable upstream revision and the manifest is
reconstructed exactly from the pinned split files, with every image verified
against its recorded digest.

\textbf{PlantDoc} \cite{singh2020plantdoc} supplies the field domain.
\NPdcAcquired{} records were acquired at a pinned source revision. Two
reductions follow, both recorded with reasons:
\NPdcExcludedDup{} images are removed by human adjudication of byte-exact
duplicate groups, and \NPdcExcludedReview{} further images are \emph{excluded,
not relabelled}, because the diagnostic evidence for a relabel was judged
insufficient and the second scientific review that would settle it has not been
performed. The exclusion records no diagnosis and asserts nothing about what
those images depict. The resulting \emph{PlantDoc Core} holds \NPdc{} images
over \NPdcClasses{} classes, split \NPdcTrain{} train / \NPdcTest{} test. No
source image was deleted and no image byte was modified.

Class support in PlantDoc Core is severely uneven---the largest training class
holds ninety times the smallest---and one arthropod-pest class has two training
images and no test images at all. It cannot be learned or measured, and is
retained only because removing it would alter the locked corpus. Table
\ref{tab:dataset-stats} gives the composition; the full provenance and leakage
counts are in the supplementary material.

\subsection{Cross-Corpus Leakage}

If a field evaluation image duplicates a laboratory training image, the
cross-domain number is contaminated. We therefore search for such pairs
exhaustively.

Candidate generation uses a 64-bit perceptual hash with exact chunked
brute-force Hamming evaluation over \emph{every} distinct-hash pair, which for
the acquired population is $1.37 \times 10^{8}$ distance evaluations. The search
is exhaustive by construction rather than index-approximate: an approximate
nearest-neighbour index would make the absence of a match an artefact of the
index's recall, which is exactly the property this audit must not have. Pairs at
Hamming distance $\leq 6$ are surfaced as candidates.

\textbf{Perceptual hashing generates candidates; it never decides.} All
\NLeakNearCore{} surfaced near pairs were adjudicated by a human as clearly
different, and the great majority are cross-class collisions on plain green
foliage---the failure mode a perceptual hash is expected to have. The audit
finds \NLeakExactCore{} exact overlaps and \NLeakUnresolved{} unresolved pairs.
No laboratory training image is a byte- or pixel-duplicate of any field
evaluation image, so the cross-domain measurement in Section \ref{sec:xdomain}
is a genuine generalisation measurement.

\textbf{Within-corpus leakage} is controlled separately. PlantVillage records
which physical leaf each image photographs, and several images can show one
leaf. The upstream train/test split is leaf-grouped; the validation split we
carve from train is \emph{also} leaf-grouped, so no physical leaf appears on
both sides of model selection. Ignoring this would let a model be selected on a
near-copy of something it trained on.

\section{Experimental Protocol}
% =========================================================================== %

\subsection{The Shared Label Space}
\label{sec:mapping}

The two corpora use different taxonomies, so cross-domain evaluation requires a
correspondence. Because that correspondence is a research degree of freedom, we
constrain it to evidence that existed before this evaluation was designed, from
exactly two sources.

\emph{Source 1: deterministic key collision.} The project's crop and disease
normalisation functions are applied \emph{unchanged and identically} to both
label sets. Eleven pairs collide on the resulting canonical key. The same
function runs on both sides, so no human judgement enters the pairing.

\emph{Source 2: a recorded human policy.} A previously approved policy assigns
every PlantDoc class of the form ``\textit{Crop} leaf'' a canonical disease of
\textit{healthy}; on the PlantVillage side the token is literal in the label.
Ten crops appear on both sides under this policy.

The result is \NSharedClasses{} shared canonical classes covering \NXdImages{}
PlantDoc Core images. Everything else is excluded \emph{with a written reason}.
The excluded set deliberately includes biologically plausible near-misses that
the normaliser does not align---for instance a gray-leaf-spot class against a
Cercospora gray-leaf-spot class, and two differently-named pepper leaf-spot
classes. Asserting those pairs would be a new semantic judgement made by the
authors of the evaluation, in a direction that would improve the headline
number. We decline, and record what a more generous mapping would change. The
mapping is conservative by construction, and Section \ref{sec:limitations}
states the consequence.

\subsection{Cross-Domain Evaluation Procedure}

A laboratory-trained model predicts over its own \NPvClasses{}-way space. To
evaluate it on the shared space, source-class probabilities are summed within
each canonical class and renormalised over the \NSharedClasses{} shared classes
only. Field images outside the mapping are \emph{excluded from the evaluation
set} rather than counted as errors, since a model cannot be faulted for a class
the protocol never asked it about.

Renormalisation discards probability mass, and how much is discarded is itself
informative, so the mean retained mass before renormalisation is recorded for
every run and reported in Section \ref{sec:xdomain}.

All \NXdImages{} shared-class Core images are used, including those PlantDoc
assigns to its training split, because a laboratory-trained model has never seen
any of them. The split composition is recorded in every result file.

\subsection{Models and Training}

Three ImageNet-pretrained \cite{deng2009imagenet} backbones span an order of
magnitude in capacity: ResNet-50 \cite{he2016resnet} (\ParamsRn{}\,M
parameters), EfficientNet-B0 \cite{tan2019efficientnet} (\ParamsEb{}\,M), and
MobileNetV3-Small \cite{howard2019mobilenetv3} (\ParamsMn{}\,M). Only the final
classifier layer is replaced. Backbone choice is the sole architectural
variable.

One protocol governs every run: $224 \times 224$ inputs, AdamW at learning rate
$3\times10^{-4}$ with weight decay $10^{-4}$, cosine decay stepped per batch,
mixed precision, batch size 64, label smoothing $0.1$, at most 15 epochs with
early stopping on validation macro-F1 at patience 3, and selection of the single
best-validation checkpoint. Training augmentation is deliberately mild:
random resized crop, horizontal flip, rotation within $\pm15^{\circ}$, and
colour jitter with \emph{near-zero hue} perturbation. Chlorosis and necrosis are
diagnosed by colour; a hue shift would destroy the signal the task depends on.

Two deviations from the common protocol are dataset-driven rather than
architectural, and both are reported rather than tuned. PlantVillage validation
is grouped by leaf identity, as described above. PlantDoc Core training uses
inverse-frequency class weighting normalised to mean one, so the effective
learning rate is unchanged; with $90{:}1$ imbalance, a class-balanced sampler
would replay the two-image arthropod-pest class roughly ninety times per epoch
and overfit it, and weighted loss is the gentler instrument.

\subsection{Metrics}

Classification is reported as accuracy, macro-F1, weighted-F1, balanced
accuracy, and macro precision and recall. Macro averages run over the classes
\emph{present in the evaluation split}, with the full declared label space
reported alongside: averaging in a zero-support class as a hard $0.0$ would
describe the split rather than the model. Predictions landing on zero-support
classes are still errors and are counted explicitly.

Probabilistic quality is reported as negative log-likelihood, Brier score, and
expected and maximum calibration error over 15 equal-width bins
\cite{naeini2015ece}. The scalar calibration error and the reliability diagram
are computed from the same bin table, so they cannot disagree. Selective
prediction \cite{geifman2017selective} is reported as a confidence--abstention
curve and the area under the risk--coverage curve.

Calibration uses a single temperature fitted by L-BFGS on validation
negative log-likelihood and applied unchanged to test and cross-domain logits.
It is never refitted on evaluation data. Temperature does not move the argmax,
so accuracy and F1 are identical before and after; only confidence moves. Both
the scaled and unscaled variants are reported, which is what makes it possible
to ask whether an in-domain calibration fix survives a domain shift.

\subsection{Statistical Methodology}
\label{sec:stats}

Two models evaluated on one dataset are not independent samples, so accuracies
are compared with \textbf{McNemar's exact test} on per-item correctness
\cite{mcnemar1947,dietterich1998tests}. The exact binomial form is used rather
than the chi-square approximation because the discordant counts here are small.
Because we report every pairwise comparison, $p$-values are additionally
reported with Holm step-down adjustment \cite{holm1979} controlling the
family-wise error rate across the whole family of tests.

Interval estimates use the \textbf{bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa)
bootstrap} \cite{efron1987bca,efron1993bootstrap} at the $95\%$ level with
\NBootstrap{} resamples. Both the bias correction and the acceleration are
computed; where either is undefined---as it is for a statistic that does not
vary across resamples---the interval degrades to a percentile interval and is
labelled as such rather than silently reported as BCa. The evaluated label space
is held fixed at the classes present in the original evaluation, so the
statistic keeps one definition across all replicates.

These two instruments answer different questions and are reported separately.
The bootstrap interval describes sampling variability of a fixed evaluation set
for one trained checkpoint; the across-seed standard deviation describes
variability of the training process itself. Neither substitutes for the other,
and a difference smaller than the seed spread is not claimed as a finding
regardless of its $p$-value.

\subsection{Reproducibility}

Every run revalidates the dataset lock (\LockDigestFull{}) before training
begins, so a checkpoint can always be traced to an exact, digest-bound corpus.
Runs are sequential on a single Apple-silicon host with a unified-memory
accelerator; timings are therefore Apple-silicon numbers and will not transfer
to server GPUs, though relative ordering between backbones should. Seeds are
set for Python, NumPy, and the framework at the start of every run, and the
validation carve-out depends only on the frame, the fraction, and the seed---not
on row order or platform.

\section{Discussion}
% =========================================================================== %

\subsection{A Saturated Benchmark Carries Little Ranking Information}

The three backbones separate cleanly on the laboratory test split, and the
separations are statistically real: every pairwise comparison there is
significant under Holm adjustment. But the spread that separates them is tiny in
absolute terms, and it does not survive the domain shift. Under the shift, the
backbone that places \emph{last} in domain is statistically indistinguishable
from the one that places \emph{first} in domain ($p = \PMcXdRnEb{}$ unadjusted,
$\PMcHolmXdRnEb{}$ after Holm), while the ordering against MobileNetV3-Small
reverses outright and is significant in both directions.

That reversal is the substantive result, and it is worth stating precisely
because the loose version of the claim is weaker than the true one.
MobileNetV3-Small is significantly \emph{better} than ResNet-50 on the
laboratory test split and significantly \emph{worse} than it under the shift.
Both legs are significant after family-wise correction. This is not a
re-ordering within noise; it is a genuine crossing.

The practical reading is uncomfortable for a literature organised around
in-domain leaderboards. Choosing a backbone by its laboratory accuracy---the
only signal available if one does not build a cross-domain evaluation---would
have selected against the architecture that transfers best. A benchmark with no
headroom cannot rank models on a property it does not measure, and by the time
accuracies exceed $0.99$, the remaining differences reflect fit to corpus-specific
regularities at least as much as disease recognition
\cite{torralba2011bias,geirhos2020shortcut}.

\subsection{In-Domain Calibration Does Not Transport}

Temperature scaling \cite{guo2017calibration} is close to free and close to
universal advice: fit one scalar on held-out validation data, apply it, obtain
better-calibrated confidences without touching accuracy. In domain, it works
here exactly as advertised.

Across the domain shift it does not merely fail to help---it actively hurts,
raising calibration error above the uncalibrated baseline for all three
backbones. The mechanism is legible rather than mysterious. Label smoothing
leaves the models mildly \emph{under}-confident in domain, so the fitted
temperature falls below $1$ and \emph{sharpens} the predictive distribution.
Sharpening is precisely right in domain, where the model is usually correct.
Out of domain the model is already over-confident relative to its much lower
accuracy, and sharpening amplifies the error it was supposed to correct.

The deployment consequence inverts the usual recommendation: a temperature
fitted on in-domain validation data should not be transported across a domain
shift, and transporting it is worse than shipping the model uncalibrated. This
is consistent with the broader finding that uncertainty quantification degrades
under shift \cite{ovadia2019trust}, but it is a sharper statement---the standard
fix does not merely lose effectiveness, it changes sign. Because temperature
does not move the argmax, a practitioner monitoring only accuracy would never
see this happen. We report both variants side by side so the comparison is
visible rather than implied.

\subsection{Why the Controls Change What Can Be Concluded}

Without the leakage audit, the honest form of our cross-domain result would have
to be ``degradation of at least this much, assuming no contamination.'' With
\NLeakExactCore{} exact overlaps established by exhaustive search and human
adjudication of every near candidate, the assumption becomes a measurement.

Similarly, without a constrained label space, a reader could reasonably suspect
the mapping had been tuned. Restricting it to a deterministic normaliser and a
pre-existing policy decision means the shared space was not chosen to produce
any particular number---and because it is conservative, its bias is in the
direction that makes our headline gap \emph{larger} than a generous mapping
would. We state that rather than leaving it for a reviewer to infer.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limitations}
% =========================================================================== %

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The two label spaces differ.} In-domain evaluation is
\NPvClasses{}-way; cross-domain is \NSharedClasses{}-way over a restricted
space. The measured drop therefore combines domain shift with a change in task
difficulty, and should be read as a paired trend across models rather than as an
isolated quantity. The trend is the claim; the absolute magnitude is not.

\item \textbf{The shared mapping is conservative by construction.} Excluded
near-misses are listed with reasons. A human-authored correspondence would
likely enlarge the shared space and change the cross-domain numbers, most
plausibly upward. Our numbers should be read as a lower bound on transfer under
a mapping that refuses to guess.

\item \textbf{The field corpus carries documented label noise.} Images whose
filenames contradict their assigned class are known to exist in PlantDoc. This
bounds achievable accuracy on it and is not corrected here, because correcting
it would alter the locked corpus and make the evaluation unreproducible against
the published lock.

\item \textbf{The field test split is small.} With \NPdcTest{} images across
27 evaluable classes, per-class metrics have wide intervals; this is why we
report bootstrap intervals rather than point estimates alone, and why per-class
claims are avoided.

\item \textbf{One class cannot be evaluated.} The arthropod-pest class has two
training images and no test images. It is neither learnable nor measurable and
is retained only for corpus integrity.

\item \textbf{Statistical scope.} McNemar tests and bootstrap intervals are
computed on seed \SigSeed{}. They characterise a fixed evaluation set for one
trained checkpoint per backbone; they do not incorporate training-process
variability, which is reported separately as across-seed spread. With three
seeds, that spread is a descriptive quantity and we do not attach significance
claims to it.

\item \textbf{Hardware.} Timing and throughput figures come from an
Apple-silicon unified-memory accelerator and do not transfer to server GPUs in
absolute terms.

\item \textbf{Cross-domain evaluation includes field-corpus training images.}
All \NXdImages{} shared-class images are used, since a laboratory-trained model
has seen none of them. This is stated explicitly and the split composition is
recorded in every result file.

\item \textbf{Governance status.} These results rest on a scientific experiment
lock that binds digests, counts, taxonomy, and splits. It is not a completed
formal dataset-governance freeze, and we do not claim otherwise.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
% =========================================================================== %

We set out to measure how much of a laboratory-trained plant disease
classifier's accuracy survives contact with field imagery, under conditions
where the measurement can be trusted. Closing the two confounds that usually sit
between such a comparison and its interpretation---train/test contamination, and
a label correspondence chosen by the people reporting the result---turns out to
change what can be concluded, not merely how carefully it must be hedged.

Two findings survive that scrutiny. First, a near-saturated in-domain benchmark
carries almost no information about which backbone transfers: the in-domain
ordering does not merely compress under the shift, it crosses, significantly and
in both directions, between the largest and smallest backbones we tested.
Selecting an architecture on laboratory accuracy is therefore not a conservative
default---it is an active bet on a property the benchmark does not measure.
Second, temperature scaling fitted on in-domain validation data degrades
out-of-domain calibration below the uncalibrated baseline, because label
smoothing drives the fitted temperature to sharpen a distribution that is
already over-confident where it matters. A practitioner watching accuracy alone
cannot observe this, since temperature never moves the argmax.

Neither finding requires a new architecture or a new corpus. Both required an
evaluation built so that the alternative explanations could be ruled out rather
than assumed away, and both point the same direction: for agricultural decision
support, the evaluation protocol deserves at least as much scrutiny as the
model. The full protocol, the dataset lock, the leakage audit, the mapping
provenance, and the generation scripts for every table and figure in this paper
are released with it.

\begin{abstract}
Plant disease classifiers trained on laboratory imagery report accuracies above
$0.99$, yet field deployment disappoints. The size of that gap is widely
asserted and rarely measured under conditions that make the measurement
trustworthy. We report a cross-domain evaluation in which the two obvious
confounds are closed rather than assumed away. First, train/test contamination:
an exhaustive perceptual-hash search over every distinct-hash pair across the
two corpora, followed by human adjudication of all \NLeakNearCore{} near
candidates, establishes \NLeakExactCore{} exact overlaps between the laboratory
training corpus and the field evaluation corpus. Second, label-space
construction: the shared \NSharedClasses{}-class space is assembled only from a
deterministic normaliser applied identically to both label sets and from a
previously recorded human policy decision, so no new semantic judgement is
introduced to make the comparison look better or worse. Three
ImageNet-pretrained backbones are trained under one protocol on the
\NPvTrain{}-image PlantVillage training split and evaluated on \NXdImages{}
field images across \NSeedsDone{} seeds, with paired significance tests and
bias-corrected accelerated bootstrap intervals, every input bound to a
digest-verified dataset lock (\LockDigest{}). Macro-F1 falls by
\RelDropFMeanRn{}--\RelDropFMeanMn{}\,\% under the shift. We find that
near-saturated in-domain accuracy carries almost no information about which
backbone transfers, because it does not produce an ordering stable enough to
carry, and that a temperature fitted on in-domain validation data, standard
practice for calibration, makes out-of-domain calibration substantially worse
rather than better.

\keywords{Plant disease recognition \and Cross-domain evaluation \and
Leakage audit \and Model calibration \and Reproducibility}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =========================================================================== %

Image-based plant disease recognition has an unusually clean success story and an
unusually persistent complaint. The success story is PlantVillage
\cite{mohanty2016plantvillage}: tens of thousands of labelled leaf photographs on
which convolutional networks reach accuracies above $0.99$, a result reproduced
so widely that the benchmark is effectively saturated. The complaint is that
these accuracies do not survive contact with a real field. PlantVillage images
are single detached leaves against uniform backgrounds under controlled lighting;
a photograph taken by a grower contains occlusion, soil, multiple leaves,
variable illumination, and a camera held at whatever distance was convenient. The
gap matters because the downstream artefact is an advisory, and a confident wrong
diagnosis becomes an unnecessary spray or a lost season.

That a gap exists is not in dispute, and field-condition corpora have been built
precisely to expose it \cite{singh2020plantdoc,moupojou2023fieldplant,wei2024plantwild}.
What is harder to find is a measurement a sceptical reader can trust, because two
confounds sit between the raw comparison and any conclusion drawn from it. The
first is contamination: if any field evaluation image is a near-duplicate of a
laboratory training image, the measured transfer is optimistic by an unknown
amount. This is not hypothetical, since such contamination has been documented in
benchmarks far more scrutinised than these \cite{barz2020purging}, and most
cross-corpus plant disease comparisons report no leakage audit at all. The second
is the label space: the two corpora do not share a taxonomy, so any cross-corpus
evaluation must construct a correspondence, and that construction is a research
degree of freedom. A generous mapping pairing biologically similar classes will
report a smaller gap than a conservative one, and the reader usually cannot tell
which was used.

This paper closes both. Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A leakage-controlled protocol.} Duplicate candidates are generated
by exhaustive perceptual-hash comparison over every distinct-hash pair rather
than by an approximate index, and every candidate is adjudicated by a human.

\item \textbf{An evidence-based shared label space} assembled from exactly two
pre-existing sources, with a written reason for every excluded class, including
biologically plausible near-misses we decline to assert.

\item \textbf{A controlled multi-seed comparison with paired statistics.} Three
backbones spanning a \ParamRatio{}$\times$ parameter range are trained under one
protocol across \NSeedsDone{} seeds and compared with McNemar's exact test on
per-item correctness \cite{mcnemar1947,dietterich1998tests} and BCa bootstrap
intervals \cite{efron1987bca}, with family-wise error control \cite{holm1979}
and every verdict recomputed independently per seed.

\item \textbf{Two findings that matter for practice.} Every pairwise verdict on
the saturated in-domain benchmark changes with the seed, and the backbone that
ranks last on two seeds ranks first on the third, so in-domain rank is not a
stable quantity from which field behaviour could be predicted. Separately, a
temperature fitted on in-domain validation data and carried across the shift
degrades calibration below the uncalibrated baseline, the opposite of the
standard recommendation.
\end{enumerate}

Where two backbones are not statistically distinguishable we say so rather than
reporting their order, and where a difference is smaller than the variation the
random seed alone induces, we do not claim it. Every input is bound to a
digest-verified dataset lock, every reported number is generated from run
artefacts by script, and no number in this document was typed by hand.

\section{Related Work}
% =========================================================================== %

\subsubsection{Corpora, laboratory and field.}
PlantVillage \cite{mohanty2016plantvillage} established both the standard
benchmark and the standard result, accuracies above $0.99$ across dozens of
crop--disease classes, on imagery whose construction from detached leaves against
uniform backgrounds is stated plainly in the original work. What followed was a
long sequence of papers reporting incremental gains on a benchmark with very
little headroom. PlantDoc \cite{singh2020plantdoc} was assembled specifically to
test whether laboratory-trained models transfer and reported that they largely do
not; FieldPlant \cite{moupojou2023fieldplant} and PlantWild
\cite{wei2024plantwild} extend the setting to larger and more varied imagery, and
PlantSeg \cite{wei2025plantseg} adds segmentation supervision. Our work is
complementary: rather than proposing a new corpus or a stronger model, we ask
what conditions a cross-corpus comparison must satisfy before its number means
anything.

\subsubsection{Dataset bias, leakage, and calibration.}
That models absorb corpus-specific regularities rather than the intended concept
predates deep learning \cite{torralba2011bias} and has been quantified
repeatedly since \cite{recht2019imagenet,geirhos2020shortcut}. For plant disease
the uniform background of laboratory imagery is an obvious shortcut candidate,
and near-duplicate leakage is the adjacent failure that \cite{barz2020purging}
showed survives in benchmarks studied for a decade. Modern networks are also
systematically over-confident, with temperature scaling the standard remedy
\cite{guo2017calibration}; under shift, uncertainty estimates degrade further and
the ranking of calibration methods changes \cite{ovadia2019trust}. Selective
prediction \cite{geifman2017selective} gives the deployment-relevant view: how
accurate a model is on the subset it will act on. We treat each as something to
measure, not to assume.

\subsubsection{Positioning within this venue.}
AgroBench \cite{shinoda2025agrobench} shows how far general-purpose
vision--language models remain from reliable agricultural judgement, and the
applied trajectory this community is on runs from detection through severity
estimation to decision support. Our contribution sits underneath all of it: an
advisory system built on a classifier selected by laboratory accuracy inherits
both the wrong architecture choice and the wrong confidence estimate, and neither
is visible from the leaderboard the choice was made on.

\section{Datasets and Leakage-Controlled Preparation}
% =========================================================================== %

\subsection{Corpora}

\textbf{PlantVillage} supplies the laboratory domain: \NPv{} colour images over
\NPvClasses{} crop--disease classes, split \NPvTrain{} train / \NPvTest{} test.
The corpus is pinned to an immutable upstream revision, with the manifest
reconstructed exactly from the pinned split files.

\textbf{PlantDoc} \cite{singh2020plantdoc} supplies the field domain.
\NPdcAcquired{} records were acquired at a pinned source revision. This differs
from the count and class total reported in the original publication, because the
distributed repository has changed since; we therefore report the pinned
revision's own numbers and bind them to a digest rather than quoting the paper's.
Two reductions follow, both recorded with reasons: \NPdcExcludedDup{} images are
removed by human adjudication of byte-exact duplicate groups, and
\NPdcExcludedReview{} further images are \emph{excluded, not relabelled},
because the diagnostic evidence for a relabel was judged insufficient and the
second scientific review that would settle it has not been performed. The
exclusion records no diagnosis and asserts nothing about what those images
depict. The resulting \emph{PlantDoc Core} holds \NPdc{} images over
\NPdcClasses{} classes, split \NPdcTrain{} train / \NPdcTest{} test. No source
image was deleted and no image byte was modified.

Class support in PlantDoc Core is severely uneven. The largest training class
holds \PdcImbalance{} times the smallest, and one arthropod-pest class has two
training images and no test images at all. It cannot be learned or measured, and
is retained only because removing it would alter the locked corpus. Full provenance and leakage
counts are in the supplementary material.

\subsection{Cross-Corpus Leakage}

If a field evaluation image duplicates a laboratory training image, the
cross-domain number is contaminated, so we search for such pairs exhaustively.
Candidate generation uses a \PhashBits{}-bit perceptual hash with exact chunked
brute-force Hamming evaluation over \emph{every} distinct-hash pair, which for
the acquired population is \NPhashComparisonsSci{} distance evaluations. An
approximate nearest-neighbour index would make the absence of a match an artefact
of the index's recall, which is exactly the property this audit must not have.
Pairs at Hamming distance $\leq \LeakThreshold{}$ are surfaced as candidates.

\textbf{Perceptual hashing generates candidates; it never decides.} All
\NLeakNearCore{} surfaced near pairs were adjudicated by a human as clearly
different, and the great majority are cross-class collisions on plain green
foliage, the failure mode a perceptual hash is expected to have. The audit
finds \NLeakExactCore{} exact overlaps and \NLeakUnresolved{} unresolved pairs.
No laboratory training image is a byte- or pixel-duplicate of any field
evaluation image, so the cross-domain measurement in Section \ref{sec:xdomain}
is a genuine generalisation measurement.

\textbf{Within-corpus leakage} is controlled separately. PlantVillage records
which physical leaf each image photographs, and several images can show one
leaf. The upstream train/test split is leaf-grouped; the validation split we
carve from train is \emph{also} leaf-grouped, so no physical leaf appears on
both sides of model selection. Ignoring this would let a model be selected on a
near-copy of something it trained on.

\subsection{Why the Two Evaluation Sets Differ in Size}
\label{sec:two-eval-sets}

Two field-corpus evaluation sets appear here and are deliberately different
sizes: cross-domain evaluation uses \NXdImages{} images, in-domain PlantDoc
testing \NPdcTest{}. What sets the size is \emph{what the model under test has
already seen}. PlantDoc's train/test boundary is a fact about PlantDoc-trained
models and carries no information about a model trained on a different corpus,
so for a PlantVillage-trained model the entire shared-class subset is
legitimately held out; restricting it would discard evidence for no
methodological gain and leave per-class supports far too thin. A
PlantDoc-trained model has seen the training split, so only the \NPdcTest{}
held-out images are admissible, and every in-domain PlantDoc figure here uses
exactly those. Neither set contains an image the model being evaluated trained
on, and the cross-domain set's composition by PlantDoc split is recorded in every
result file, so a reader preferring the more restrictive choice can see which
images it would remove.

\section{Experimental Methodology}
% =========================================================================== %

\subsection{The Shared Label Space}
\label{sec:mapping}

The two corpora use different taxonomies, so cross-domain evaluation requires a
correspondence. Because that correspondence is a research degree of freedom, we
constrain it to evidence that existed before this evaluation was designed, from
exactly two sources.

\emph{Source 1: deterministic key collision.} The project's crop and disease
normalisation functions are applied \emph{unchanged and identically} to both
label sets. Eleven pairs collide on the resulting canonical key. The same
function runs on both sides, so no human judgement enters the pairing.

\emph{Source 2: a recorded human policy.} A previously approved policy assigns
every PlantDoc class of the form ``\textit{Crop} leaf'' a canonical disease of
\textit{healthy}; on the PlantVillage side the token is literal in the label.
Ten crops appear on both sides under this policy.

The result is \NSharedClasses{} shared canonical classes covering \NXdImages{}
PlantDoc Core images. Everything else is excluded \emph{with a written reason}.
The excluded set deliberately includes biologically plausible near-misses that
the normaliser does not align, for instance a gray-leaf-spot class against a
Cercospora gray-leaf-spot class, and two differently-named pepper leaf-spot
classes. Asserting those pairs would be a new semantic judgement made by the
authors of the evaluation, in a direction that would improve the headline
number. We decline, and record what a more generous mapping would change; Section
\ref{sec:limitations} states the consequence.

\subsection{Cross-Domain Evaluation Procedure}

A laboratory-trained model predicts over its own \NPvClasses{}-way space. To
evaluate it on the shared space, source-class probabilities are summed within
each canonical class and renormalised over the \NSharedClasses{} shared classes
only. Field images outside the mapping are \emph{excluded from the evaluation
set} rather than counted as errors, since a model cannot be faulted for a class
the protocol never asked it about. Renormalisation discards probability mass,
and how much is discarded is itself informative, so the mean retained mass before
renormalisation is recorded for every run and reported in Section
\ref{sec:xdomain}. All \NXdImages{} shared-class Core images are used, for the
reason given in Section \ref{sec:two-eval-sets}.

\subsection{Models and Training}

Three ImageNet-pretrained \cite{deng2009imagenet} backbones span an order of
magnitude in capacity: ResNet-50 \cite{he2016resnet} (\ParamsRn{}\,M
parameters), EfficientNet-B0 \cite{tan2019efficientnet} (\ParamsEb{}\,M), and
MobileNetV3-Small \cite{howard2019mobilenetv3} (\ParamsMn{}\,M). Only the final
classifier layer is replaced. Backbone choice is the sole architectural
variable.

One protocol governs every run: $224 \times 224$ inputs, AdamW at learning rate
$3\times10^{-4}$ with weight decay $10^{-4}$, cosine decay stepped per batch,
mixed precision, batch size 64, label smoothing $0.1$ held fixed across every
run, at most 15 epochs with early stopping on validation macro-F1 at patience 3,
and selection of the single best-validation checkpoint. Augmentation is
deliberately mild: random resized crop, horizontal flip, rotation within
$\pm15^{\circ}$, and colour jitter with \emph{near-zero hue} perturbation, since
chlorosis and necrosis are diagnosed by colour. The label-smoothing choice
matters for the calibration result (Section \ref{sec:calibration}).

Two deviations are dataset-driven rather than architectural, and both are
reported rather than tuned. PlantVillage validation is grouped by leaf identity,
as described above. PlantDoc Core training uses inverse-frequency class weighting
normalised to mean one, so the effective learning rate is unchanged; at
$\PdcImbalance{}{:}1$ imbalance a class-balanced sampler would replay the
two-image arthropod-pest class roughly \PdcImbalance{} times per epoch and
overfit it, and weighted loss is the gentler instrument.

\subsection{Metrics}

Classification is reported as accuracy, macro-F1, weighted-F1, balanced
accuracy, and macro precision and recall. Macro averages run over the classes
\emph{present in the evaluation split}, with the full declared label space
reported alongside: averaging in a zero-support class as a hard $0.0$ would
describe the split rather than the model, and predictions landing on such classes
are still errors and are counted explicitly. Probabilistic quality is reported as
negative log-likelihood, Brier score, and expected and maximum calibration error
over 15 equal-width bins \cite{naeini2015ece}, with the scalar error and the
reliability diagram computed from the same bin table so they cannot disagree.
Selective prediction \cite{geifman2017selective} is reported as a
confidence--abstention curve and the area under the risk--coverage curve.
Calibration uses a single temperature fitted by L-BFGS on validation negative
log-likelihood and applied unchanged to test and cross-domain logits, never
refitted on evaluation data; both variants are reported, which is what makes it
possible to ask whether an in-domain fix survives a domain shift.

\subsection{Statistical Methodology}
\label{sec:stats}

Two models evaluated on one dataset are not independent samples, so accuracies
are compared with \textbf{McNemar's exact test} on per-item correctness
\cite{mcnemar1947,dietterich1998tests}, in the exact binomial form rather than
the chi-square approximation because the discordant counts here are small. Since
we report every pairwise comparison, $p$-values are also reported with Holm
step-down adjustment \cite{holm1979} controlling the family-wise error rate
across the whole family of tests.

A paired test computed on one training run is a statement about that run, so
every McNemar test is recomputed independently on each seed's own checkpoints
and Holm-adjusted within that seed, and verdicts are reported as holding in all
seeds, in none, or in some. We do not pool $p$-values across seeds: three runs
is far too few for a meta-analytic combination, and the question that matters is
whether a verdict is stable. Inline $p$-values are quoted from seed \SigSeed{};
per-seed stability is tabulated in Section \ref{sec:indomain}.

Interval estimates use the \textbf{bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa)
bootstrap} \cite{efron1987bca} at the $95\%$ level with
\NBootstrap{} resamples, computed on seed \SigSeed{}. Where either the bias
correction or the acceleration is undefined, as it is for a statistic that does
not vary across resamples, the interval degrades to a percentile interval and is
labelled as such. The evaluated label space is held fixed at the classes present
in the original evaluation, so the statistic keeps one definition across all
replicates.

The two instruments answer different questions. The bootstrap interval describes
sampling variability of a fixed evaluation set for one checkpoint; the
across-seed standard deviation describes variability of the training process
itself. A difference smaller than the seed spread is not claimed as a finding
regardless of its $p$-value.

\subsection{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:repro}

Every run revalidates the dataset lock (\LockDigestFull{}) before training
begins, so a checkpoint can always be traced to an exact, digest-bound corpus.
Runs are sequential on a single Apple-silicon host with a unified-memory
accelerator; timings are therefore Apple-silicon numbers and will not transfer
to server GPUs, though relative ordering between backbones should. Seeds are set
for Python, NumPy, and the framework at the start of every run, and the
validation carve-out depends only on the frame, the fraction, and the seed,
never on row order or platform. This version reports \NRunsDone{} runs covering
seeds \SeedList{}.

\textbf{A silent mixed-precision failure.} Under fp16 autocast the framework
skips the optimiser step on non-finite gradients only on CUDA, so on the Metal
backend used here one overflowing gradient left a parameter permanently NaN
without raising anything. We added the same step skip on the Metal path; the
guard leaves a run that never overflows numerically identical, verified by
re-training a completed run to a byte-identical checkpoint, so runs before and
after remain comparable, and seed \SigSeed{} was verified unaffected. Runs that
skip a step are identified rather than pooled silently: \SkippedStepsNote{}.
Every run was screened for non-finite parameters and for the constant-prediction
signature a NaN model produces; none other than that one shows either. The
supplement gives the full diagnosis.

\section{Cross-Domain Generalisation}
\label{sec:xdomain}
% =========================================================================== %

Transferred to field imagery, the same checkpoints lose roughly three quarters
of their macro-F1. Relative degradation is \RelDropFRn{}\,\% for ResNet-50,
\RelDropFEb{}\,\% for EfficientNet-B0, and \RelDropFMn{}\,\% for
MobileNetV3-Small, against a leakage audit that establishes \NLeakExactCore{}
exact overlaps between the two corpora. This is a generalisation measurement,
not a contamination artefact. Absolute drops are in the supplementary table.

Absolute cross-domain performance is poor for every backbone (Table
\ref{tab:cross-domain-compact}). The bootstrap intervals on the
\NXdImages{}-image evaluation set are narrow relative to the collapse itself: on
seed \SigSeed{} the macro-F1 point estimates are \MacroFXdRnS{},
\MacroFXdEbS{}, and \MacroFXdMnS{}, with intervals \CiFXdRn{}, \CiFXdEb{}, and
\CiFXdMn{}. The magnitude is therefore not an artefact of a small evaluation
set.

\subsection{What the Ranking Does and Does Not Support}

Two of the three pairwise comparisons are stable across every seed. Both larger
backbones beat MobileNetV3-Small under the shift, significantly on each seed
after family-wise correction ($p = \PMcXdRnMn{}$ and $\PMcXdEbMn{}$ on seed
\SigSeed{}, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}} = \PMcHolmXdRnMn{}$ and $\PMcHolmXdEbMn{}$), with
margins several times the across-seed standard deviation.

The comparison between the two larger backbones supports nothing. It is not
significant on any seed ($p = \PMcXdRnEb{}$, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}} =
\PMcHolmXdRnEb{}$, on \NDiscXdRnEb{} discordant items), the sign of the
difference changes between seeds, and the mean margin is an order of magnitude
below the seed spread. We therefore decline to order them, and note that a paper
reporting a single seed could have ordered them either way with equal apparent
justification.

Our ordering claim is therefore a \emph{partial} one: MobileNetV3-Small below
both larger backbones. The full three-way ordering reproduces in no setting,
cross-domain included, because it inherits the instability of the pair we
decline to order, which is why the supplementary ranking table marks every
setting unstable.

Set against the in-domain picture, the asymmetry is the result. In domain, where
the benchmark is saturated, no ordering survives reseeding, not even the partial
one. Under the shift the partial ordering reproduces on every seed. The
laboratory benchmark is not merely a weak predictor of field ranking; it does
not produce a stable ranking to predict from.

\subsection{How the Models Fail}

The failure is not refusal but misallocation. Every shared class receives
predictions from every backbone, and none is left unpredicted; instead the
models over-assign a small number of frequent laboratory classes, with the most
heavily over-predicted class absorbing several times its true share of the
evaluation set. Mean probability mass retained inside the shared space before
renormalisation is \RetainedMassRn{}, \RetainedMassEb{}, and \RetainedMassMn{}:
a third or more of each model's belief falls on classes the protocol never asked
about, which is itself a symptom of the shift rather than of the mapping.

\section{Calibration and Selective Prediction}
\label{sec:calibration}
% =========================================================================== %

In domain, temperature scaling behaves exactly as advertised. The fitted
temperature is remarkably consistent across backbones and seeds, \TempPvRn{},
\TempPvEb{}, and \TempPvMn{}, and it reduces expected calibration error by an
order of magnitude (Table \ref{tab:calibration-compact}).

\input{tables/table6_calibration_compact.tex}

Carried across the domain shift, the same scalar makes calibration worse. For
every backbone and every seed, nine cases without exception, cross-domain ECE
rises rather than falls. This is not a marginal degradation: the calibration
error after applying the standard remedy is \EceRatioRn{}, \EceRatioEb{}, and
\EceRatioMn{} times what it was without any calibration at all. It is also the
most uniform result in the paper, nine model--seed cases moving the same
direction, from a fitted temperature whose across-seed standard deviation never
exceeds \TempSdMax{}.

Selective prediction confirms that the confidence signal degrades rather than
merely shifting: the area under the risk--coverage curve rises from
\AurcPvInRn{} in domain to \AurcXdRn{} cross-domain for ResNet-50, the other
backbones behaving similarly. Abstention buys far less accuracy out of domain
than in it, because the confidences it depends on have stopped being
informative.

\section{Discussion}
% =========================================================================== %

\subsection{A Saturated Benchmark Carries Little Ranking Information}

On seed \SigSeed{} all three laboratory comparisons are significant, which is why
a single-seed reading of these same runs would have reported a clean separation.
That separation is an artefact of which seed is displayed, as Figure
\ref{fig:seed-spread} shows directly.

Of the nine pairwise verdicts in Table
\ref{tab:significance-across-seeds-compact}, three hold on every seed, two fail
on every seed, and four depend on the seed. All three laboratory comparisons
fall in the seed-dependent group. The fourth is MobileNetV3-Small against
ResNet-50 on the field test split, and it is seed-dependent for a different
reason: the margin is consistent in sign on every seed and more than three times
the across-seed spread, but \NPdcTest{} test images leave one seed short of the
Holm threshold. Every verdict that reproduces on all seeds places
MobileNetV3-Small last, while ResNet-50 and EfficientNet-B0 are indistinguishable
in both field settings with the sign changing between seeds. The result is
therefore not that the in-domain ordering inverts under the shift, but that
there is no stable in-domain ordering for the shift to invert.

The practical reading is uncomfortable for a literature organised around
in-domain leaderboards. Choosing a backbone by laboratory accuracy, the only
signal available without a cross-domain evaluation, ranks MobileNetV3-Small above
ResNet-50 on two of the \NSeedsDone{} seeds, when under the shift it is
significantly worse than ResNet-50 on all three. By the time accuracies exceed
$0.99$ the remaining differences reflect fit to corpus-specific regularities at
least as much as disease recognition
\cite{torralba2011bias,geirhos2020shortcut}.

\subsection{In-Domain Calibration Does Not Transport}

Temperature scaling \cite{guo2017calibration} is close to free and close to
universal advice: fit one scalar on held-out validation data, apply it, obtain
better-calibrated confidences without touching accuracy. In domain it works here
exactly as advertised. Across the domain shift it does not merely fail to help,
it actively hurts.

The mechanism is legible. Label smoothing leaves the models mildly
\emph{under}-confident in domain, so the fitted temperature falls below $1$ and
\emph{sharpens} the predictive distribution. Sharpening is precisely right in
domain, where the model is usually correct. Out of domain the model is already
over-confident relative to its much lower accuracy, and sharpening amplifies the
error it was supposed to correct. That mechanism also bounds the claim: what we
demonstrate is that the standard pipeline as commonly assembled, label smoothing
followed by in-domain temperature fitting, yields a scalar that is harmful under
shift. Whether a model trained without label smoothing would fit a temperature
above $1$ and degrade more gracefully is a separate question this experiment
does not settle (Section \ref{sec:limitations}).

The deployment consequence inverts the usual recommendation: a temperature
fitted on in-domain validation data should not be transported across a domain
shift, and transporting it is worse than shipping the model uncalibrated. This
is consistent with the broader finding that uncertainty quantification degrades
under shift \cite{ovadia2019trust}, but sharper: the standard fix does not
merely lose effectiveness, it changes sign. Because temperature does not move
the argmax, a practitioner monitoring only accuracy would never see it happen.

Both findings depend on the controls. Without the leakage audit the honest form
of the cross-domain result would be ``degradation of at least this much, assuming
no contamination''; with \NLeakExactCore{} exact overlaps established by
exhaustive search and human adjudication of every near candidate, the assumption
becomes a measurement. And because the shared space comes from a deterministic
normaliser and a pre-existing policy rather than from us, its conservative bias
makes the headline gap \emph{larger} than a generous mapping would.

% =========================================================================== %
\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limitations}
% =========================================================================== %

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The two label spaces differ, and the mapping is conservative.}
In-domain evaluation is \NPvClasses{}-way, cross-domain \NSharedClasses{}-way
over a restricted space, so the measured drop combines domain shift with a change
in task difficulty and should be read as a paired trend across models rather than
as an isolated quantity: the trend is the claim, the absolute magnitude is not.
Excluded near-misses are listed with reasons; a human-authored correspondence
would likely enlarge the shared space and move the numbers, most plausibly
upward, so ours are a lower bound on transfer under a mapping that refuses to
guess.

\item \textbf{The calibration result is scoped to a label-smoothed pipeline.}
Every run uses label smoothing $0.1$, which is why the fitted temperature falls
below $1$ and sharpens rather than softens. The finding is therefore about the
standard pipeline as commonly assembled rather than about temperature scaling in
isolation; an ablation without label smoothing would separate the two and is the
obvious next experiment.

\item \textbf{The field corpus carries documented label noise.} Images whose
filenames contradict their assigned class are known to exist in PlantDoc. This
bounds achievable accuracy on it and is not corrected here, because correcting
it would alter the locked corpus and make the evaluation unreproducible against
the published lock.

\item \textbf{The field test split is small, and so is the design.} With
\NPdcTest{} images across \NPdcTestClasses{} evaluable classes, per-class metrics
have wide intervals, which is why we report bootstrap intervals, avoid per-class
claims, and why one otherwise stable verdict falls short of significance on one
seed. Three seeds and three backbones likewise make the ranking-instability
finding a demonstration that in-domain rank \emph{can} fail to transfer under a
controlled protocol, not an estimate of how often it does. Bootstrap intervals
use seed \SigSeed{} alone and no test incorporates training-process variability,
reported separately as across-seed spread, to which we attach no significance
claim. Timing figures come from an Apple-silicon accelerator and do not transfer
to server GPUs in absolute terms.

\item \textbf{Cross-domain evaluation includes field-corpus training images.}
All \NXdImages{} shared-class images are used, since a laboratory-trained model
has seen none of them (Section \ref{sec:two-eval-sets}). Scoring only PlantDoc's
own test split would give a much smaller set with wider intervals; the split
composition is recorded in every result file so that alternative can be
reconstructed.

\item \textbf{Governance status.} These results rest on a scientific experiment
lock binding digests, counts, taxonomy, and splits. It is not a completed formal
dataset-governance freeze, and we do not claim otherwise.
\end{enumerate}

% =========================================================================== %
\section{Conclusion}
% =========================================================================== %

Closing the two confounds that usually sit between a cross-corpus comparison and
its interpretation changes what can be concluded, not merely how carefully it
must be hedged. Two findings survive that scrutiny. First, a near-saturated in-domain benchmark
carries almost no information about which backbone transfers, because it does not
produce an ordering stable enough to carry: across \NSeedsDone{} seeds every
pairwise verdict on the laboratory split changes with the seed, and the backbone
that ranks last on two seeds ranks first on the third. The cross-domain partial
ordering separating the smallest backbone from the other two reproduces on every
seed. Selecting an architecture on laboratory accuracy is therefore not a
conservative default; it is an active bet on a property the benchmark does not
measure. Second, temperature scaling fitted on in-domain validation data degrades
out-of-domain calibration below the uncalibrated baseline, because label
smoothing drives the fitted temperature to sharpen a distribution already
over-confident where it matters, and a practitioner watching accuracy alone
cannot observe it.

Neither finding requires a new architecture or a new corpus. Both required an
evaluation built so that the alternative explanations could be ruled out rather
than assumed away, and both point the same direction: for agricultural decision
support, the evaluation protocol deserves at least as much scrutiny as the model.
The protocol, dataset lock, leakage audit, mapping provenance, and generation
scripts for every table and figure are released with the paper.
